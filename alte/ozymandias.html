4. Ozymandias of Egypt — Percy Bysshe Shelley

Summary. A traveller describes the broken statue of Ozymandias in a vast desert: two trunkless legs stand near a shattered face. The sculptor has preserved the ruler’s arrogant expression, and the pedestal boasts of the king’s mighty works. Yet no empire remains in sight—only the ruined monument and endless sand. The boast becomes ironic: political power decays, while art and the poem preserve its memory.

Notable details. The poem is a sonnet framed as the traveller’s report; the speaker did not claim to see the ruins firsthand. The sculptor captured the “sneer of cold command.” “Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!” is written on the pedestal, but “Nothing beside remains.” The textbook title adds “of Egypt” to the familiar poem title Ozymandias.

Longer answers to practise

What is the central idea of the poem? Ozymandias calls himself “King of Kings” and orders the powerful to admire his works. Yet his statue lies broken, and empty sand surrounds it. Time has outlasted his empire. The sculptor's skill preserves the king's proud expression, but cannot preserve his rule. The poem questions pride in political power.

Explain the irony of the inscription. The pedestal commands the mighty to look upon Ozymandias's works and despair. The traveller sees no mighty works nearby: only broken stone and “lone and level sands.” The words meant to proclaim lasting greatness now reveal how completely that greatness has vanished.
