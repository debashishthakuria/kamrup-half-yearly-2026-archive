1. A Cup of Tea — Katherine Mansfield

Summary. Wealthy Rosemary Fell meets a hungry young woman, Miss Smith, outside an antique shop. Instead of giving her tea money, Rosemary takes her home, delighted by the idea of playing the generous heroine. She feeds her guest, but her husband Philip calls Miss Smith “astonishingly pretty.” Rosemary’s jealousy displaces her charity: she gives the girl money and sends her away. Her final question to Philip—“am I pretty?”—reveals her insecurity and the limits of her kindness.

Notable details. Rosemary admires an enamel box priced at twenty-eight guineas but initially leaves it in the shop. Miss Smith asks only for the price of a cup of tea. Rosemary takes three five-pound notes after putting two of five notes back; the story does not directly show the handover, though Rosemary says she gave Miss Smith money. Its contrasts are luxury versus hunger, and professed charity versus vanity.

Longer answers to practise

Why does Rosemary take Miss Smith home? Rosemary is moved by the girl's hunger, but she also likes the thought of doing something dramatic and generous, like a scene from a novel. Her invitation has kindness in it, yet she imagines telling her friends about it. The story makes us question whether she sees Miss Smith as a person or as a part in her own adventure.

How is the ending ironic? Rosemary has spoken of helping a poor woman and of sisterhood. But when Philip praises Miss Smith's beauty, Rosemary's concern turns to jealousy. She sends the girl away with money and asks Philip whether she herself is pretty. The woman who seemed secure in her wealth ends up seeking reassurance about her looks.
