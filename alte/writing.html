Passage: Philosophy of Education

Philosophy of Education is a label applied to the study of the purpose, process, nature and ideals of education. It can be considered a branch of both philosophy and education. Education can be defined as the teaching and learning of specific skills, and the imparting of knowledge, judgment and wisdom, and is something broader than the societal institution of education we often speak of.

Many educationalists consider it a weak and woolly field, too far removed from the practical applications of the real world to be useful. But philosophers dating back to Plato and the Ancient Greeks have given the area much thought and emphasis, and there is little doubt that their work has helped shape the practice of education over the millennia.

Plato is the earliest important educational thinker, and education is an essential element in "The Republic" (his most important work on philosophy and political theory, written around 360 B.C.). In it, he advocates some rather extreme methods: removing children from their mothers' care and raising them as wards of the state, and differentiating children suitable to the various castes, the highest receiving the most education, so that they could act as guardians of the city and care for the less able. He believed that education should be holistic, including facts, skills, physical discipline, music and art. Plato believed that talent and intelligence is not distributed genetically and thus is be found in children born to all classes, although his proposed system of selective public education for an educated minority of the population does not really follow a democratic model.

Aristotle considered human nature, habit and reason to be equally important forces to be cultivated in education, the ultimate aim of which should be to produce good and virtuous citizens. He proposed that teachers lead their students systematically, and that repetition be used as a key tool to develop good habits, unlike Socrates' emphasis on questioning his listeners to bring out their own ideas. He emphasized the balancing of the theoretical and practical aspects of subjects taught, among which he explicitly mentions reading, writing, mathematics, music, physical education, literature, history, and a wide range of sciences, as well as play, which he also considered important.

During the Medieval period, the idea of Perennialism was first formulated by St. Thomas Aquinas in his work "De Magistro". Perennialism holds that one should teach those things deemed to be of everlasting importance to all people everywhere, namely principles and reasoning, not just facts (which are apt to change over time), and that one should teach first about people, not machines or techniques. It was originally religious in nature, and it was only much later that a theory of secular perennialism developed.

During the Renaissance, the French skeptic Michel de Montaigne (1533 - 1592) was one of the first to critically look at education. Unusually for his time, Montaigne was willing to question the conventional wisdom of the period, calling into question the whole edifice of the educational system, and the implicit assumption that university-educated philosophers were necessarily wiser than uneducated farm workers, for example.

Passage A: A school reading corner

Rina: Why are these books in the corridor, Kabir?
Kabir: Our class is setting up a reading corner. The library closes before some bus students can visit it.
Rina: Where will the books come from?
Kabir: Teachers have donated twenty books, and students may lend one each. We will write the owner's name inside every borrowed book.
Rina: Who will look after them?
Kabir: Two volunteers will take turns at lunch. They will record loans in a notebook and return the books on Fridays.
Rina: Can anyone use the corner?
Kabir: Yes, but the books must remain in good condition. Our aim is access, not a competition over who reads most.

  1. Why is the class setting up a reading corner? (2)
  2. Give two sources of books. (2)
  3. How will the volunteers manage loans? (2)
  4. What is Kabir's aim, and what must readers do? (2)
  5. Find words in the passage meaning (i) people who offer help without being required to; (ii) reach or opportunity to use something. (1 + 1)
Show answers
  1. Some bus students cannot visit the library before it closes, so the class is creating another place to read.
  2. Teachers donate books, and students lend books.
  3. Two volunteers take turns at lunch, record loans in a notebook and return books on Fridays.
  4. He wants access rather than a reading contest; readers must keep the books in good condition.
  5. (i) volunteers; (ii) access.

Passage B: Choosing a course

Mother: You seem uncertain about your course application, Neel.
Neel: I enjoy biology, but my friends think I should study engineering.
Mother: Have you asked a teacher about your options?
Neel: Yes. Sen Ma'am suggested that I compare the subjects and talk to people who work in both fields.
Mother: That sounds sensible. Is the deadline close?
Neel: It is next Monday. I will visit the college website tonight and write down my questions.
Mother: What matters most to you?
Neel: I want a course that suits my interests and abilities, not merely one that sounds fashionable.
Mother: Then make a careful choice. We can discuss the costs together tomorrow.

  1. Which subject does Neel enjoy? (1)
  2. Why does he feel pulled towards engineering? (1)
  3. What two steps did Sen Ma'am suggest? (2)
  4. What will Neel do tonight and when is the deadline? (2)
  5. Explain Neel's view of a suitable course. (3)
  6. Find a word meaning “reasonable and practical.” (1)
Show answers
  1. Biology.
  2. His friends think he should study engineering.
  3. Compare the subjects and speak to people working in both fields.
  4. He will visit the college website and note his questions tonight; the deadline is next Monday.
  5. It should match his interests and abilities; he does not want to choose merely because a course is fashionable.
  6. sensible.

Passage C: A cleaner festival

Tara: Will the festival committee still give out plastic cups?
Imran: No. We have arranged steel cups that can be washed and reused.
Tara: Who will wash them during the event?
Imran: Volunteers will collect them at two return desks. A local group will wash them in clean water behind the hall.
Tara: What about the food stalls?
Imran: Stall owners have agreed to keep separate bins for food scraps and other waste. We will put clear signs above the bins.
Tara: Will all this be expensive?
Imran: We must pay a deposit for the cups, but we expect to get it back after returning them. Reducing waste is worth the effort.

  1. What will replace plastic cups, and why? (2)
  2. Where can visitors return used cups? (1)
  3. Who washes the cups and where? (2)
  4. What will stall owners do with waste? (2)
  5. Why does Imran expect to recover the deposit? (2)
  6. Find a word meaning “used again.” (1)
Show answers
  1. Washable, reusable steel cups will replace disposable plastic cups.
  2. At two return desks.
  3. A local group will wash them in clean water behind the hall.
  4. They will use separate bins for food scraps and other waste.
  5. The committee expects the deposit back when it returns the cups.
  6. reused.

Passage D: Sport and study

Coach: Why did you miss practice yesterday, Maya?
Maya: I had a science test this morning and needed extra revision. I should have informed you earlier.
Coach: Thank you for saying so. How can you manage both next week?
Maya: I can revise for an hour before dinner and attend practice on Tuesday and Thursday.
Coach: The match is on Saturday. Can you come for a short session on Friday too?
Maya: Yes, if it ends by five. My bus leaves at five fifteen.
Coach: Agreed. We will practise passing, not play a full match. Remember to rest on Friday night.
Maya: I will. I want to play well without neglecting my studies.

  1. Why did Maya miss practice, and what does she admit she should have done? (2)
  2. On which two days can she attend regular practice? (2)
  3. Why must Friday's session end by five? (1)
  4. What will the team practise on Friday instead of playing a full match? (1)
  5. How does Maya plan to balance sport and study? Give three details. (3)
  6. Find a word meaning “not giving enough attention to.” (1)
Show answers
  1. She revised for a science test and admits she should have informed the coach earlier.
  2. Tuesday and Thursday.
  3. Her bus leaves at five fifteen.
  4. Passing.
  5. She will revise before dinner, attend two regular practices and join a short Friday session; she also intends to rest.
  6. neglecting.
