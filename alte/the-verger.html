3. The Verger — W. Somerset Maugham

Summary. Albert Edward Foreman has served for sixteen years as verger of St Peter’s, Neville Square. The new vicar learns that he cannot read or write and demands that he learn within three months or leave. Foreman resigns. While walking home, he finds a street without a tobacco shop and opens one himself. His tobacconist-and-newsagent business expands to ten shops and substantial savings. When a bank manager wonders what he could have become if literate, Foreman replies that he would have remained the verger. The irony celebrates practical judgement while exposing the vicar’s narrow measure of ability.

Notable details. The new vicar praises Foreman’s work but regards illiteracy as an unacceptable risk. Foreman notices the opportunity when he wants a cigarette and cannot find a shop. He learns to sign his name after entering business; he does not become fully literate. After ten years his deposit is a little over £30,000. The ending is ironic, not an argument that schooling is worthless.

Longer answers to practise

Why did Foreman lose his job despite serving well? The new vicar discovers that Foreman cannot read or write. Although Foreman has served St Peter's faithfully for sixteen years, the vicar considers his illiteracy an unacceptable risk. He gives Foreman three months to learn. Foreman refuses and resigns, choosing dignity rather than accepting the condition.

Explain the last line. The bank manager wonders what Foreman might have become had he been literate. Foreman replies that he would still be verger at St Peter's. If he had kept that job, he would never have noticed the empty market for a tobacco shop. The ending turns a setback into an unexpected opportunity without saying that education itself is useless.
