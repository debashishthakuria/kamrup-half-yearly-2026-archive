6. Strange Meeting — Wilfred Owen

In brief. A soldier seems to escape battle through a deep tunnel. In a gloomy hall, he meets another dead soldier. The stranger tells him about the hopes, beauty and truth that war cut short. He wanted people to understand “the pity of war,” but violence silenced him. At the end, he says he is the enemy the speaker killed the day before. He calls the speaker “my friend” and asks them both to sleep. The poem shows the suffering shared by men who have been made enemies.

Remember. The meeting happens in a place the speaker recognises as Hell. The enemy is not a monster: he had hopes and gifts of his own. “The pity of war” matters more to Owen than tales of glory. The last words, “Let us sleep now,” suggest death, rest and an end to fighting.

Longer answers to practise

What makes this meeting strange? The speaker meets a soldier he killed only yesterday. In life they fought on opposite sides; after death the man calls him “my friend.” Their conversation reveals shared hopes and suffering. The enemy is no longer a faceless target but a human being whose future was taken away.

How does Owen show the pity of war? The dead soldier remembers the beauty and truth he wished to seek and share. War ended those possibilities. His words about blood, wounds and lost progress challenge the idea that war brings honour. Even the killer and the killed can understand each other, but only after both are dead.
