5. Because I Could Not Stop for Death — Emily Dickinson

Summary. The speaker imagines Death as a courteous carriage companion who stops for her; Immortality also rides with them. They pass a school, fields of grain, and the setting sun. The mood darkens as the sun passes them and the air turns cold. They pause at a house resembling a swelling in the ground—a grave. Looking back after centuries, the speaker recalls realizing that the horses were travelling towards Eternity. The poem presents death as a journey, but its chill and final uncertainty complicate the initial courtesy.

Notable details. Death is personified as “kindly” and marked by “Civility”; the carriage contains the speaker, Death and Immortality. The three passing scenes can suggest stages of life, though that is an interpretation rather than an explicit label in the text. In the original poem the speaker corrects herself: “Or rather—He passed Us—” after saying they passed the setting sun. The barely visible roof and buried cornice identify the “House” as a grave; “Gossamer” and “Tulle” show how lightly she is dressed against the cold.

Longer answers to practise

What do the scenes on the journey suggest? The school with children, fields of grain and setting sun may suggest childhood, maturity and the close of life. The poem does not label the stages outright; the speaker observes them as the carriage moves slowly on. When the sun passes them and the air grows cold, Death's gentle manner feels less comforting.

Why is the house important? The “House” is a grave, suggested by its swelling ground, scarcely visible roof and cornice in the earth. Death's polite carriage ride pauses there. From that point the speaker speaks as someone who has been dead for centuries, looking back on a journey that leads toward Eternity.
